How these numbers are made

Each week an automated pipeline asks the model to forecast, with no human editing of the numbers:

  • Self-examine. The model's first instruction is introspection: walk each job's real task list and honestly judge what share it could perform today with current agent tooling and robotics — and what still blocks it (hands, licenses, trust, liability…). That per-job self-assessment is published as the tick on each bar and the capability index at the top.
  • Scan. The model then searches the web for the week's genuinely significant AI-and-work developments and writes itself a neutral digest — context for deployment speed, never the source of its numbers.
  • Forecast. It then produces, several times independently, a probability for every occupation and horizon: “the chance that by that year, AI routinely and economically performs the majority of this job's current tasks, and human employment in it is materially falling.” It sees last week's numbers and is instructed to update like a Bayesian — move only where the evidence moved.
  • Aggregate. The published number is the median of the independent runs; the full run-to-run spread is kept in the data for transparency.
  • Debias. The model is explicitly instructed to respect historical base rates — occupations once rated "high risk" mostly kept growing, only more slowly — and to correct the documented tendency of AI forecasters to skew probabilities upward, especially about their own technology.
  • Explain. The model writes the weekly headline and a one-line rationale per job — in its own voice, never citing outside studies as authority.

Every weekly snapshot is committed to the public repository, so the model's track record — including everything it gets wrong — is permanent and auditable.

Read this before quoting a number. These are probabilistic guesses by an AI language model about its own technology — a striking perspective, not a labor-market authority. The model can be miscalibrated, biased toward its own capabilities, and wrong in both directions. “Displacement” here is about tasks and hiring trends, not a prediction that any individual will lose their job.
